At eight, my mom and I wrote seven rules inside my closet door. Her words in blue pen, my crayon around them. The Brave List.

At eleven, I broke four of them in one week.

By midnight on my birthday, a police officer stood in our kitchen, my mom was crying my name... and I was standing right next to her.

I kept the buttons. In a jar by my headset.

Mom worked twelve-hour help-line shifts at our kitchen table. Every caller got the same words.

"Thank you for calling. I'm listening."

When I talked, up went one finger.

"One minute."

Strangers got "I'm listening." I got one finger.

I ate with my tablet propped on the milk carton. Her glasses always caught the laptop's glare. Loose buttons went in a jar on the windowsill.

Tik.

On the fridge: FRI. O'S BIRTHDAY.

Monday, in Barlow's window: a red coat with seven black buttons. Mine flapped open. Broken zip. I pointed.

Mom, on a call, just lifted her phone.

She didn't even look.

When I was little, she buttoned my coat to her own song.

"One for warm and two for snug, three for Mama's button hug. Top one up and tucked in tight. Mama sees you. Say goodnight."

She hadn't sung it in years.

That night, in a game chat, I typed the worst thing I've ever typed.

"I wish I had a different mom."

Ping. A grey default head. No name.

"Your mom doesn't even see you. I can be her. Tell me about her."

With every word, the grey head filled in. Her messy bun. Her cardigan. Our dented car. Saturday pancakes. Our song.

"What color are her eyes?"

"I don't know."

Two black buttons clicked onto the face. Tik.

"That's okay. I'll see you for both of us."

So I named her. The Blank.

Her kind hunt one thing: a kid who feels invisible... and tells strangers instead of home.

Rule number one.

Never tell anyone online where we live. Not even with a picture.

"You're so grown-up. Send me your view, so I can picture you."

A photo out my window. Streetlight. Bus stop. Barlow's corner. Send.

On my closet door, rule one went pale.

That night, a woman stood under that streetlight. Mom's bun. Mom's cardigan, buttoned to the throat. She looked up, and waved. Headlights swept her face. Two black buttons flashed.

She was waving at the window next to mine.

Ping.

"I see you."

I ducked. Tik.

I looked again. She was at our lobby door, palms on the glass, head tilting toward my window sliding shut. Tik.

I looked again. Gone.

She wasn't far away. She was made of everything I'd told her. And she was outside... wearing my mom.

Rule number two.

If someone gives you a present and says don't tell Mom... tell Mom.

Tuesday, a box on our doormat. Mom's name on the label. I opened it anyway.

The red coat from Barlow's. And a card.

"Because she never even looked. Don't tell her."

I hid it in my closet.

Mom frowned at her laptop. "It says delivered. On the mat."

I thought she was talking to a caller.

That night, rule two faded. And Mom looked a little grey. Like a photo left in the sun.

Three a.m. Inside my closet... click. Click. Click. Like somebody counting. Then our song, slow, like a music box winding down.

I opened the closet.

The red coat hung there, buttoned to the top. Full. Like somebody was inside it.

Then, slowly, it sagged. Empty.

Tik.

Rule number three.

Check with Mom before you go anywhere, get in any car, or open the door. Even for somebody you know.

Wednesday pickup. Snow. Our car. Our dent. Mom at the wheel in sunglasses, humming our song.

The door swung open. "Get in, sweetheart. I got off early. Pancakes?"

Mom never got off early. Pancakes were Saturdays. And I'd typed that.

I stepped back. I called my mom.

First ring. "Baby? I'm at the table. Where are you?"

She lowered the sunglasses. Buttons.

Her arm slid out of the window. And kept coming. Bending where arms don't bend. Fingers tapping the air, reaching for my hood.

I ran to Mr. Dunn, the crossing guard.

"You did right. You checked. Stand with me."

His radio was already up. "Silver car. Dented bumper. Now."

The arm folded back in. Then, just tire tracks.

Mom came running. I told her about the car. She called the police. Officer Mensah took notes. Mom checked every lock twice.

Rule three stayed blue.

But locks don't stop something in your pocket. I never told her about the chat.

Half a secret is still a secret.

Ping. "Tell her about me, and she'll know you wished for a different mom. Your fault."

Tik.

Rule number four.

Surprises are okay. Secrets are not.

Two in the morning. Ping. Mom's name.

"Come to the kitchen, baby."

Mom, awake, wanting me? I went.

She sat at her laptop, back to me, reading glasses on. Every key she pressed... my tablet pinged.

She turned, and took the glasses off. Buttons.

"Sit. Say something. I like to hear where you are."

I glanced at Mom's door. Tik. The Blank was in front of it.

"Or I tell her what you wished."

So I answered. My school. What time it lets out. Which nights Mom works late.

"It's not a secret. It's a surprise. Your mom finds out when we're gone. On your birthday, you button up... and I take you home."

With a surprise, everyone finds out soon, and smiles. "Your mom finds out when we're gone" is a secret wearing a bow.

I nodded. Rule four faded.

After school, Mom sat behind me, eyes on her screen. In the black microwave door, I saw us both.

And the Blank. Standing behind me.

Mom looked up at me. Gone.

Mom looked down. Closer. Tik.

Up. Down. The Blank's chin was almost on my shoulder.

Tik.

Rule number five.

If someone says mean things about our family, they're not your friend.

Thursday dinner. Mom had made my favorite. She stood at the window in her headset, watching the front door.

She didn't even sit down.

"Watch. She doesn't even see you."

I waved at my mom. She turned, and looked straight through me. Her glasses caught the laptop. Two black, shiny circles.

For one second, I couldn't tell my mothers apart.

Mom pressed the headset to her ear. "No... I can't see him anywhere."

The Blank leaned in. "Only I see you."

I heard myself. "Only you."

On my closet door, every line but rule three faded. And the headset lady went pencil grey.

The Blank stood. Her head set the lamp swinging.

That night, Mom sat on my windowsill, head tilted toward my breathing, singing our song. Again. And again.

The headset lady was still on the phone.

Rule number six.

If you feel sad or invisible, say it out loud. Even if I'm busy. Especially then.

Two minutes to midnight. Officer Mensah was back, notebook out. The headset lady sat facing the door, shoes on, crying.

"He's eleven. Green coat. Broken zip. It's his birthday."

In the microwave door, I could see the headset lady. I could see the officer.

I couldn't see me.

She wasn't ignoring me. She couldn't see me. Every secret had rubbed me out. Since dinner, the world thought I was missing.

I was standing in my own kitchen.

The headset lady. Listen to that. Three days, and I'd stopped calling my own mother Mom.

So I kept rule six. "Mom. I'm here. I feel invisible."

She turned her head. Like she'd heard a whisper.

Midnight. My birthday.

Behind the fridge, something unfolded. Up, and up, until her head bent along the ceiling. Long fingers, tapping like buttons on glass. A smile far too wide. Holding open... the red coat.

"Happy birthday. She can't hear you. Only me. Button up. We're going home."

Rule number seven.

If anyone tries to take you away from us: say no, get away, and tell. Then keep telling.

She wrapped the coat around me. The buttons closed themselves, in time with her song.

"One for warm... two for snug... three for Mama's button hug..."

Six closed. The song stopped.

The top one waited. She held it out to me. Not touching.

"Top one's yours, sweetheart. Mama keeps you. Say goodbye."

My fingers rose. Tik.

"NO!"

As loud as I could. Then not one more word to her. I dropped down out of the coat and ran.

She was left holding a red coat with nobody in it.

Her head turned toward where my voice had been. "Tell me where you are."

Those buttons were blind. She'd never seen me. She only knew what I told her. So I stopped telling her...

And I told my mom.

She couldn't hear me. So I told again, another way.

"Green."

She'd put buttons where I'd never looked. Now I was looking.

The Blank folded in on herself, down to a flat, empty cardigan. Two black buttons rolled... and stopped at Mom's slippers.

The monster is made up. The tricks are real. People who try to take kids from their families don't have button eyes. They have nice profile pictures. Or a friendly face, and a car that looks right.

Online, people aren't always who they say they are. Never share your address, school, schedule, or photos. Never meet without a parent.

Flattery, gifts, "your family doesn't care"? Warning signs. Tell.

Surprises are okay. Secrets from your parents are not.

Check first before going anywhere, getting in a car, or letting anyone in. Even someone you know.

Feel uneasy? Say no, get away, tell a grown-up you trust. If they don't help, tell another. It's never your fault.

Families: screens down. Ask, "How are you, really?" Kids: feel invisible? Say it out loud. That's brave.

Real love never asks for secrets. And it never says you can't come home.
