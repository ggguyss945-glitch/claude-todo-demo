If a delivery driver ever rings your doorbell at three in the morning and asks for a signature, do not sign for it. Not on his screen. Not on paper. Not with your finger on the glass. Don't say anyone's name out loud. And if the package starts knocking, remember that it isn't asking to get out. It's asking to get in.

My father walked out on us when I was nine. That's what everyone said. Last night, someone tried to deliver him back.

It's 3:07 in the morning when your phone lights up.

"Your package has been delivered."

You haven't ordered anything. You're still reading the notification when the doorbell rings.

You open the doorbell camera. A delivery driver is standing under your porch light. Brown uniform. Brown cap. A tablet in one hand. He's looking straight into the lens, like he knows exactly where it is.

Next to his boots there's a box. Tall and narrow, wrapped in brown tape. It's almost as tall as he is.

At the curb, a white van idles with its hazard lights on. On the side, in faded letters, it says NIGHTLINE DELIVERY. You've never heard of them.

The driver smiles at the camera. He doesn't stop smiling.

"Signature required," he says. "I just need someone to sign."

You open the delivery app. There's a new order in your account that you didn't place. One item. Signature required. Recipient: this address. Under sender, there's only one word.

RETURN.

You press the button on the doorbell and tell him you didn't order anything.

"It's not an order," he says. "It's a return. Something that belongs to this house is coming back. Somebody just has to sign for the exchange."

Exchange. You don't like that word. An exchange means something comes in, and something goes out.

If this ever happens to you, there are seven rules. The fourth one is the rule I broke without knowing it.

Rule number one. Don't open the door to sign.

He'll tell you the screen can't read a signature through glass. He'll hold the tablet up to the camera so you can see the empty line. He'll say it only takes a second.

On your phone, the tablet fills the frame. The signature line isn't empty. The first letter of your name is already there, in your handwriting. The pen stroke stops halfway, like someone was interrupted.

"Just finish it," he says. "It's easier when you finish it."

You notice something else. When he talks, his mouth moves a little too late. The words come first. The lips catch up a moment after. In the corner of the screen, the timestamp flickers. 3:09. 3:09. 3:08.

You keep the door locked. A signature on that screen is a key. Don't give him one.

The driver lowers the tablet. He looks down at the box, then back up at the lens.

"No problem," he says. "We'll just make sure it's going to the right person."

Rule number two. Don't say anyone's name out loud.

A return needs a label. The label needs a name. He doesn't have one yet, so he'll try to get one from you.

He tilts the box so the label faces the camera. The "to" line is blank. He taps it with his finger.

"Who's the lucky one tonight?" he asks. "You? Your mom? Anyone else asleep upstairs?"

You almost tell him to leave your family out of this. You almost say their names. You don't.

He waits. He keeps smiling. His smile is wider now than it was a minute ago, and it hasn't moved, not even a little.

"That's okay," he says. "Names come out eventually. They always do."

Your phone buzzes in your hand.

"Delivery photo added."

Rule number three. If the delivery photo shows the inside of your house, don't turn around.

You open the photo.

It's a picture of you. From behind. You're standing in your front hallway in your socks, holding your phone, lit by the glow of the screen. It was taken from the far end of the hallway. The timestamp says 3:11.

It's 3:11 now.

Behind you, something sets a ring of keys down in the bowl on the side table. You know that sound. Your father used to come home late and drop his keys in that bowl, every night, the same little jingle.

"Hey, buddy," a voice says behind you. "Why are you up so late?"

Proof of delivery only counts when the delivery is received. If you turn around and look at what's standing in your hallway, you've received it. So you don't. You keep your eyes on the doorbell camera. On the porch, the driver tilts his head, like he's listening to the same voice you are.

You count to thirty. The hallway goes quiet. When you finally check the photo again, it's gone. The app says it was never there.

That's when you hear footsteps on the stairs.

Rule number four. Don't let anyone else sign for you.

Your mother comes down the stairs in her robe, squinting at the light. "Who's at the door at this hour?" she asks.

You show her the camera. She sees the van at the curb, the hazard lights, the faded letters on the side. And all the color drains out of her face.

"That's the van," she whispers. "That's the same van."

You've never heard this story. She tells it to you now, standing in the dark hallway, without taking her eyes off the screen.

The night your father left, twenty-one years ago, a delivery van stopped outside at three in the morning. A driver rang the bell. There was a box on the porch, tall and narrow, and the box was talking. It had his own father's voice. His father had died the year before.

Your mother told him not to go out. He said he'd just sign for it and bring it in. She heard the door open. She heard the van door slide shut. In the morning, the box on the porch was empty, and your father was gone.

Everyone said he walked out on his family. Your mother never told anyone about the box.

On the porch, the driver leans close to the camera. He's talking to her now.

"Ma'am," he says, "he's been waiting a long time to come home. You're on the account. Your signature works just as well."

Your mother's hand goes to the door chain.

You catch her wrist. Any signature from this house counts. Not just yours. Anyone who lives here can sign for the exchange, and whoever signs is the one who goes out.

She lets go of the chain. Her hand is shaking.

The driver's smile doesn't change. But on the camera, for half a second, his eyes catch the porch light and shine white, like an animal's on the side of the road.

Then the box knocks.

Rule number five. Don't answer the box.

Two knocks. A pause. One more.

Your mother makes a sound you've never heard from her. That was your father's knock. Two, then one. He used to knock like that on your bedroom door every morning to wake you up for school.

"Mar?" says a voice from inside the box. Your father's voice. "Mar, it's cold in here. Let me in."

Your mother presses both hands over her mouth.

Here's what you need to understand about the box. It isn't trying to get out. It's trying to get in. Whatever is inside it learned your father's voice over twenty-one years. It knows your mother's nickname. It knows the knock. It's using all of it to get one of you to open the door.

"Buddy?" the voice says. "Buddy, I'm sorry I left. I didn't mean to. Just sign, and I can come home."

You don't answer. You hold your mother, and you don't answer.

The knocking gets faster. Two, then one. Two, then one. Two, then one.

On the camera, the driver isn't smiling anymore. His face is perfectly still. His head turns slowly toward the side of the house, toward the backyard, as if he just remembered something.

Then he looks back at the lens and smiles again, wider than before.

"No signature," he says. "That's okay. I'll leave it at the door."

Rule number six. If he leaves the package, don't bring it inside before sunrise.

He sets the box against your front door. You hear it scrape across the welcome mat. He takes a photo of it with his tablet. Then he walks back to the van and climbs inside.

The app updates. "Delivery attempted. Package left at door. Return pickup scheduled for 6:12 a.m."

Sunrise is at 6:12.

Anything you carry over the threshold counts as accepted. So the box stays outside. All night, it knocks against the door, right at the height of your chest. Two, then one. Your mother sits on the floor of the hallway with her back to the wall, and you sit next to her. Neither of you says anything.

At 3:40, the knocking stops.

You look at the camera. The box is still there against the door. But the van is still at the curb. The hazard lights are still blinking. And the driver's seat is empty.

Rule number seven. If the knocking stops and the van is still outside, check every window in the house.

The driver gave up on the front door. That doesn't mean he gave up.

You run through the house. Kitchen window, locked. Living room, locked. Bathroom, locked. You take the stairs two at a time. Your mother's room, locked.

The door at the end of the hall is open a crack.

Your little sister's room. Lily is eight. She sleeps with a nightlight shaped like a moon, and her window faces the side of the house.

You push the door open.

The window is wide open. The curtains are moving. Her bed is empty, and the blankets are still warm.

Your phone buzzes in your hand.

"Return complete."

You open the app. There's a new delivery photo. It was taken from below, looking up at the side of your house, at 3:41. Lily is leaning out of her open window in her pajamas. The driver is holding his tablet up to her. Her small finger is on the screen.

Under the photo, the signature reads, in a child's handwriting: Lily.

She was awake the whole time. She heard the knock through her bedroom vent. Two, then one. She heard a voice say it was her daddy, and that he was cold, and that he just needed someone to sign so he could come home. She never even knew him. She only wanted to help.

Through her window, you see the van's hazard lights stop blinking. The van pulls away from the curb with its headlights off, and it turns the corner, and it's gone.

At 6:12 the sun comes up. Your mother opens the front door.

The box is still on the porch. The tape has been cut. It's empty, except for a ring of keys. Your father's keys, rusted, still on the keychain you made him in second grade.

The police took a report. They said kids run away. They said she'd turn up.

You haven't slept since.

Tonight, at 3:07, your phone lights up again.

"Your package is on its way. Signature required."

You open the app. There's one item. Recipient: this address.

And under sender, in a child's handwriting, it says: Lily.

Somewhere down the street, you can already hear it. Small knuckles on cardboard. Two knocks. A pause.

And one more.
