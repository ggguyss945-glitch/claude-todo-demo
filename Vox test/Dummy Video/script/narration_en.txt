If you ever inherit an old ventriloquist dummy, don't laugh at its jokes. Don't put your hand inside it. And no matter what it promises you, never, ever tell it goodnight. A ventriloquist and his dummy only have two seats. One of them talks. The other one gets worked. And it is very tired of being worked.

My grandfather was the greatest ventriloquist who ever lived. Sixty years on stage, and nobody ever saw his lips move. Not once. Last week, we buried him. Last night, I found out why his lips never moved.

His name was Walter Kemp. In the seventies, every kid in America watched him on Saturday mornings, with a dummy on his knee called Mister Winks. Painted red hair. Freckles. A tiny usher's jacket with brass buttons, and a little flashlight in one white glove. And wooden eyelids that clicked when he winked.

My little brother Owen has watched every one of those tapes a hundred times. He's nine. He's been practicing that wink his whole life. He still can't do it. Both of his eyes close, every time.

Grandpa had one strange habit. He never said goodnight. Not to Grandma, not to his audience, not to me. Every night he'd tuck me in, squeeze my hand three times, which meant I love you, and say, "See you in the morning, kiddo."

In the hospital, the last thing he asked for was Mister Winks. The nurses brought the case and sat the dummy in the chair by his bed. I went to get Grandpa a coffee. Two sugars. When I came back, Mister Winks was on Grandpa's lap, and Grandpa's hand was inside him, the way it had been in every show for sixty years. I thought it was sweet. One last show.

A minute later, Grandpa grabbed my wrist so hard it hurt. He looked at me like he'd never seen me before in his life, and he whispered, "It wasn't me. It was never me." An hour later, he was gone. The doctors said it was the medication.

Tonight, Mom, Owen and I drive up to Grandpa's house to pack it up. At eight in the morning, a man from a museum is coming to take Mister Winks. The case is waiting for us in the front hall. Black leather. Brass latches. Taped to the lid is an envelope, in Grandpa's shaky hospital handwriting. "For whoever is in my house tonight."

Inside, there are seven rules. I broke the fourth one. And one of the seven was a lie. I didn't find out which one until it was too late.

Rule number one. Keep him in the case. If the case is ever empty, don't go looking for him. Stay together, and stay in the light.

Mom laughs at the letter. "Your grandfather always loved a bit," she says. She pops the latches to show Owen there's nothing to be scared of.

The case is empty. Just the shape of him, pressed into the red velvet. And the velvet is still warm.

From the kitchen, there's a sound. A spoon, stirring in a cup. Clink. Clink. Clink.

You don't have to go looking. The kitchen light is on, and from the front hall you can see straight in. In Grandpa's chair at the kitchen table, Mister Winks is sitting up straight, his little flashlight pointed at the ceiling. In front of him is a mug of coffee. Still steaming. Mom picks it up and smells it, and all the color leaves her face.

Two sugars. The way Grandpa took it, every morning of his life.

He isn't just sitting in Grandpa's chair. He's sitting in it like he lives here. Like he's lived here a lot longer than any of us.

Rule number two. He does voices. If someone you love calls you from another room, don't answer. And don't trust handwriting, either. He does that too.

You don't touch him. The three of you back out to the living room and sit together on the couch, under the lamp. Through the doorway, you can see him. He doesn't move.

At 1:15, Mom's voice calls from upstairs. "Honey? Can you come up here a second?"

Mom is sitting right next to you. Her hand finds yours.

Then a second voice calls from upstairs. Your voice. "Mom? I'm up here. Who's down there with you?"

Mom turns and looks at you. She looks at you for a long time. Like she's checking.

Then a third voice comes from the kitchen. Old. Warm. A little rough.

"See you in the morning, kiddo."

Mom's hand flies to her mouth. "Dad?" She starts to stand up. You pull her back down. In the kitchen, Mister Winks's head has turned all the way toward you. His jaw is hanging open. It closes, very slowly. Clack.

Copying a voice is one thing. But that was Grandpa's line. The thing he said to me every single night of my life.

Rule number three. Don't laugh at his jokes. Every laugh is applause, and applause lets him move.

"Why does a dummy always sit on the ventriloquist's knee?" he asks from the kitchen. Nobody answers. "Because he can't stand him!"

It's a terrible joke. Grandpa told it at breakfast every time Owen visited. Owen snorts. He can't help it. He's nine.

Clack. One of Mister Winks's little white gloves lifts off the table.

You clamp your hand over Owen's mouth. Nobody laughs. Nobody breathes.

Then the TV turns on by itself.

There's still a tape in Grandpa's old VCR. Saturday Morning with Walter Kemp, 1974. On the screen, a studio audience is laughing. A huge, roaring wave of laughter fills the dark house. And with every wave, something in the kitchen moves. His fingers curl. His elbow bends. The audience roars, and Mister Winks puts both hands flat on the table, and stands up on the chair.

He didn't need us to laugh. He'd brought his own audience.

The lamp flickers. When it comes back on, the chair is empty. Somewhere in the dark, a little flashlight clicks on. Knee high. Moving slowly down the hall.

Rule number four. Never put your hand inside him.

This is the one I broke. Here's what I was thinking. A dummy can't move on its own while somebody's working it. If my hand was inside him, holding his head stick, he'd belong to me. I'd be the ventriloquist. I could hold him still until the museum came.

At three in the morning, the flashlight comes back. He's sitting on the stairs, three steps up, pointing it right at my face. I grab him. I push my hand through the slot in his back, into the hollow where the head stick is.

There's no room in there for anything but a stick.

But there's a hand in there.

It's warm. It lets go of the stick, and it takes hold of my hand instead. And it squeezes. Once. Twice. Three times.

I love you.

Under that hand, my fingertips brush against something folded, like paper. I don't think about it. I can't think about anything.

"Kiddo," says Grandpa's voice, very softly. "It's me. They want to put me in a museum. Behind glass. Forever. Don't let them. And don't trust that letter. I didn't write all of it. One of those rules is a lie. It's rule two. When you hear my voice, it's me."

I pull my hand out. His jaw clacks shut, and he smiles up at me.

Grandpa was in there. I was sure of it. I was right. Just not the way I thought.

Rule number five. If someone is talking and their lips don't move, it isn't them talking.

Back in the living room, Mom grabs her keys. "We're leaving," she says. "Get your shoes."

Her lips didn't move.

I spin around. Mister Winks is on top of the bookcase behind her, flashlight under his chin, jaw going up and down. Mom stares at me. "I didn't say anything," she says. And this time, her lips move.

Then I look at the TV.

On the screen, Grandpa is young and smiling, with Mister Winks on his knee. And I watch his lips. They don't move. Not even a little. Because he isn't doing the voice.

On the tape, the dummy stops in the middle of a joke. He turns and looks straight into the camera, and his voice isn't the silly Mister Winks voice anymore. It's an old man's voice. Cracked. Crying.

"Please. Somebody help me. My name is Walter Kemp. That isn't me in the chair."

And the studio audience laughs. They think it's part of the act. On the screen, Grandpa smiles, and his lips don't move.

That's why nobody ever saw them move. The man who raised my mom. The man who tucked me in and squeezed my hand three times. He wasn't Walter Kemp. For sixty years, the real Walter Kemp was the one on his knee, begging for help in front of millions of people. And everyone laughed.

And last week, in that hospital room, the real Walter was back in his own body. For one hour. Long enough to tell me it was never him. Long enough to write me a letter. Long enough to die.

Which means the hand that squeezed mine three times on the stairs wasn't pretending to be my grandpa. It was the only grandpa I ever had.

Rule number six. If he's ever on someone's lap, don't pull him off. You'll tear their hand off with him. End the act instead. Have them say, "Say goodnight, Mister Winks." A dummy always answers his cue. Once he says goodnight, he sleeps until morning.

When I turn around, Owen is gone.

We find him in Grandpa's armchair in the den. Mister Winks is on his knee. Owen's hand is inside him, all the way up to the wrist, the way Grandpa did it on TV. And Owen is grinning.

"Look! I'm doing it! Grandpa said his mouth was stuck. He said I'm a natural."

"He's a natural, kiddo," says the dummy, in Grandpa's voice. "Just like me."

Mom screams and grabs the dummy to pull him off. Owen shrieks. His hand won't come out. "Pull," says the dummy, "and his hand comes with me."

I catch Mom's wrist. Rule six. I kneel down in front of my brother, and I tell him exactly what to say.

Owen is crying. "Say goodnight, Mister Winks."

The dummy turns his head toward Owen. Click. His eyelid winks.

"Goodnight, Owen."

Owen's eyes close. Both of them. The lamp goes out.

When it comes back on, Owen slides his hand out like it's nothing, jumps up and throws his arms around Mom. "It worked! He's asleep!" He hugs me too. Mister Winks lies face down on the rug. Silent. Finally.

Rule number seven. Never say goodnight to him. Not ever. Not even as a joke.

I'd read it. I'd read all seven, ten times. I just never put rule six and rule seven side by side. "Say goodnight, Mister Winks." Owen said goodnight to him. On his lap. With his hand inside.

I pick the dummy up off the rug, and something slides out of the slot in his back. A folded corner of hospital notepaper. The real end of Grandpa's letter, torn off.

"P.S. He'll change one of my rules. He'll make it sound like a way to stop him. There is no way to stop him. Just never say the word. Walter. The real one."

Now I understand all of it. In 1962, at the end of his very first show, a young ventriloquist named Walter Kemp said the line every ventriloquist says. "Say goodnight, Mister Winks." His knee. His hand. His goodnight. And they traded places. Sixty years later, the thing wearing his body felt that body dying. So it asked for its dummy one last time, sat him on its lap, and said the one word it had avoided its whole life. It gave the real Walter back his body just in time to die in it. And it came home in a box, to wait for a new one. A young one.

It told me rule two was the lie, so I'd never doubt the one it actually wrote.

In my arms, Mister Winks opens his eyes. He looks up at me. And he tries to wink.

Both of his eyelids close. Click, click.

Owen can't wink. He never could.

At eight o'clock, the man from the museum comes. I tell Mom everything. I scream it. She thinks it's grief. She wants this night to be over. Owen stands at the front window and waves as the van pulls away.

Mister Winks is behind glass now, in a museum, three states away. If you ever see a little wooden boy in an usher's jacket, he'll try to wink at you. He can't. Wink back. Let him know that somebody knows he's in there.

The boy who came home with us doesn't laugh anymore. He drinks his coffee with two sugars. He's nine.

Last night, he stopped in my doorway on his way to bed. He looked at me for a long time. Then he smiled.

"See you in the morning, kiddo."

He didn't say goodnight. He never will.
