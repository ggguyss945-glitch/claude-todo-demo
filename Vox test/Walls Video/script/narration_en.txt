If your little brother says someone lives in the walls... don't laugh.

If the armchair is warm in the morning, and nobody sat in it... don't say it's the heater.

And if your dad stands in your bedroom doorway at three in the morning, in his red flannel shirt, head tilted, just watching you sleep... don't say goodnight.

Your dad is asleep.

My little brother Theo had seven rules for our new house. He was six. He couldn't spell yet, so he made me write them down, word for word, in his dinosaur notebook. I was twelve. I thought they were the silliest rules I had ever written.

Every grown-up in our house had an explanation for every one of them.

Every explanation was wrong.

By morning, there would be three police cars on our lawn, a hole in my closet wall, and a man with a dog's face sitting on our curb in handcuffs, telling the officers that we were the ones living in his house.

We moved into fourteen Larkspur Lane in August. A heat wave. Fans in every window.

The old owner, Mr. Pell, had just walked away from the place. Left everything. His big green armchair. His curtains. A stone frog by the front step, with a key under it.

"Handy," Dad said, and left it there.

The garage had a keypad. It still opened on Mr. Pell's old code. Dad said he'd change it this weekend.

On the kitchen counter, there was a note for us, in shaky handwriting.

"Welcome home. A few things about the old girl. She settles at night. The pipes knock. Mice come in the fall. One hall switch does nothing. The attic is sealed, don't bother. Be happy here. L. Pell."

Dad read it out loud, and laughed. "Well, that's every noise explained."

That first night, I woke up at three. Dad was standing in our doorway. Red flannel. Head tilted. Just watching us sleep.

"Night, Dad," I said.

He didn't answer. He just backed away, slowly, down the dark hall.

In the morning, Dad couldn't find his red flannel shirt. He said it got lost in the move.

The next night, Theo told us about Hushabye.

"He lives in the wall," Theo said. "He sings hush-a-bye, hush-a-bye. He has a sad dog face."

Mom and Dad looked at each other. Because they knew where a sad dog face came from.

Dad's old Halloween mask. A rubber hound. Long droopy ears. Heavy, sad eyes. Real teeth. It gave Theo nightmares for a year. So Dad taped it into a box, wrote GONE FOREVER on the lid, and put it on the top shelf in the garage.

"It's a nightmare, buddy," Dad said. "The mask is in the garage. Taped shut."

Nobody checked.

That's when Theo made his rules.

Okay. Before rule two, I need one word from you, in the comments.

When something knocks inside your wall at night, what is it?

Ghost, or pipes?

One word. Choose fast. That's what everybody does.

Rule number one.

Lock every door, every night. Even the garage door. Even the little basement window.

So Mom did. Front door. Back door. The door from the garage into the kitchen. Every night, she checked every lock, and Theo checked behind her.

And every morning, one door was unlocked. Always the same one. The door from the garage into the kitchen.

"You forgot," Dad told Mom.

On the fifth night, I went down for water at two in the morning. The kitchen was dark. And I watched the deadbolt on the garage door turn. Slowly. All by itself.

Click.

Unlocked.

From the kitchen side.

There was nobody in the kitchen. Nobody I could see.

Rule number two.

Count the cookies. If something is missing, say it out loud. Don't say mice.

Things went missing. Half a loaf of bread. Four yogurts. Theo's chocolate cereal. A whole roast chicken, the night after Mom bought it.

"Mice," said Dad. "The note said."

"Mice don't twist the bread bag shut," I said.

Because that's what we found, every time. The bag, folded neatly, twisted shut again. Like someone polite had been eating our food.

That night, I heard chewing. Inside the pantry wall. Slow. Careful.

There was a crack in the plaster, behind the cereal boxes. Just wide enough for light. I leaned in.

An eye leaned in on the other side.

A heavy, droopy, sad eye. In a face made of rubber.

It blinked.

And then it was gone, and something was crawling away inside the wall, fast, along the whole kitchen, up toward the ceiling.

I told Dad. Dad said I was dreaming. The note said mice.

Rule number three.

If the big chair is warm, and nobody sat in it, tell.

Mr. Pell's green armchair. Every morning, it was warm. Warm like someone had just stood up.

"Vent's right under it," Dad said.

It was August. The heat had been off since June.

I lifted the seat cushion. Under it, folded very small, were Theo's drawings. The ones that kept falling off the fridge. We never found them. We thought Theo lost them.

Every drawing was the same. A tall man, inside a wall, with a sad dog face. In a red shirt.

Somebody had been taking them down. So that we would never see all of them together.

Rule number four.

Never open the hall closet at night. Never look in the walls alone.

Remember the hall switch that does nothing?

On Saturday, Mrs. Dabrowski from across the street brought us a pie. She was nice. She was nosy. "Your attic light's on every night," she said. "Right around two."

"We don't have an attic light," Mom said. "It's sealed."

That night at two, I stood in the upstairs hall, and I flipped the switch that does nothing.

Somewhere above me, in a place that was sealed, a light came on. A thin yellow line, around the edge of the hatch, inside the hall closet.

And a voice in the ceiling, right above my head, whispered,

"Off."

I wanted to open that closet so badly. Rule four. Not alone. Not at night.

I didn't open it.

I flipped the switch off. I walked, I did not run, to my parents' room, and I told my dad everything.

Dad sighed. "Old wiring, Sam."

Rule number five.

Know your address. Sing it.

Theo had a song. "Fourteen Larkspur Lane. Maple Falls. That's my house. That's my name." He sang it fifty times a day. In the bath. At dinner. In his sleep. It drove us crazy.

That night, I heard it through the wall by my bed.

But not in Theo's voice.

In a man's voice. Slow. Low. Wrong.

"Fourteen Larkspur Lane... that's my house."

Then the knocking started. And the voice in the wall started talking. And it said things I had heard before.

"Just the pipes. Old houses settle. It's only mice. Go back to sleep."

It wasn't Dad's voice.

They were Dad's words.

No. They were the note's words. Every excuse my dad had given us, all summer, was written down for him on day one. By the man who wanted us to have an excuse for every sign he left.

Rule number six.

If someone is inside, don't look for him. Get away from him, get behind a locked door with a phone, and call.

That night, at three, I woke up.

Dad was standing in our doorway. Red flannel. Head tilted. Watching us sleep.

"Night, Dad," I almost said.

And then, from down the hall, through my parents' open door... I heard my dad snore.

The man in the doorway stepped into our room.

The moonlight hit his face. Long, droopy ears. Heavy, sad eyes. A wet, black, rubber nose. And under it, a mouth that wasn't rubber at all. Human teeth.

He knelt down beside Theo's bed. And he tucked Theo in. Gently. Like he'd done it a hundred times.

"Hush-a-bye," he whispered.

Then the dog face turned, very slowly, toward me. And he lifted one finger to where his lips should be.

"Shhh. Just the pipes."

He stood up, and he went into my closet. I heard the back wall of my closet slide. Then nothing.

I did not scream. I did not follow him. I didn't look for him.

I picked Theo up, blanket and all, with my hand over his mouth, and I walked down that hall, past the closet, where the door had opened just a crack. Just enough to watch me go.

I didn't look. I kept walking.

Mom and Dad's door. I slipped in, shut it, locked it, and shook my dad awake.

"Sam, it's just the..."

"Dad," I said. "Your shirt is upstairs. He's wearing it."

My dad looked at my face. And for the first time all summer, he didn't explain anything.

Mom grabbed her phone and dialed nine-one-one.

"What's the address of your emergency?"

And my mom, in a brand new house, at three in the morning... couldn't remember it.

Theo could.

"Fourteen Larkspur Lane, Maple Falls," he sang into the phone. "That's my house. That's my name."

"Stay in that room," the dispatcher said. "Keep the door locked. Officers are two minutes away. Stay on the line with me."

So we stayed. And the man in the walls came looking for us.

Scratching, inside the bedroom wall. Moving around the room. Behind the dresser. Behind the headboard. Then the vent by the floor rattled, and through the grille, very softly, a voice sang,

"Hush-a-bye. That's my house."

Then sirens. Blue light on the ceiling. Heavy boots on the stairs.

The officers found him in a space between my closet and Theo's. Dead space, they called it. A gap the builders left, sixty years ago. A hidden space, a foot and a half wide, with a sleeping bag, Dad's flashlight, our bread bags folded in a neat pile, and a box from our garage that said GONE FOREVER. Empty.

He was wearing the mask, and my dad's red flannel shirt.

When Officer Reyes pulled the mask off, there was no monster under it. Just a tired old man with grey stubble, blinking in the flashlight.

"Lawrence Pell," she said.

He never walked away. He walked out the front door for the movers, and right back in through the garage. With the key under the frog. And his old code on the keypad. He had lived in our walls for four months.

On the way to the car, he looked up at my window.

"That was my room," he said. "When I was a boy."

The next morning, I found the pencil marks on my doorframe. L, age four. L, age eight. L, age twelve.

And one more, higher than all of them. Fresh pencil.

Dated last week.

Rule number seven.

There was no rule seven.

Theo told it to me that first night, and I didn't write it down. I said we ran out of room. Every night, Theo tapped the empty line at the bottom of the page, and I never asked him why.

Rule seven was two words.

Believe me.

Okay. Breathe. You can turn a light on now.

None of this happened. There's no Larkspur Lane, no Mr. Pell, and no hound mask in our garage. Sam and Theo were made up by someone who got up at two in the morning to check a noise... and found a fan, and felt very silly. Feeling silly is fine.

Because most noises really are the house. The only way to catch the one that isn't, is to stop explaining signs away.

So here's the real list.

Lock every door and window, every night, including the garage door and the basement.

When you move in, change the locks and the codes. No key under the frog.

Food gone, things moved, doors unlocked, lights you didn't touch? Tell your family, and check together, in daylight.

If you think someone is inside, don't look for him. Get out, or lock yourself in a room with a phone, and call nine-one-one.

Know your address by heart.

And grown-ups: when a kid says something's wrong, listen. Then check.

Now. Go back to the word you left before rule two.

Ghost, or pipes?

Both of them let you go back to sleep. There was a third word I didn't give you.
