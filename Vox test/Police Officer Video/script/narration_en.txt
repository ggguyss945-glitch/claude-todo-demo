If a police officer ever knocks on your door after midnight and tells you the homeowner has reported a stranger inside, do not step outside to explain.

Especially if the house belongs to you.

Keep your identification inside. Be careful what you say. And if the officer asks you to prove you live there, remember that somebody else may be listening to your answers.

It's 12:36 in the morning when the knocking starts.

Through the peephole, you see a police officer standing under your porch light. His uniform looks ordinary. His badge has a number. There's a patrol car at the curb, and a second officer is sitting behind the wheel.

"Sorry to disturb you," he says. "We've had a report of an unauthorized person at this address."

You tell him you live here.

He touches the camera on his chest.

"Could you say that again?"

You repeat yourself, a little louder.

He nods, then looks toward the car.

"That's what the other gentleman said."

Only then do you notice someone sitting in the back seat.

His head is lowered. His hands are folded in his lap. One thumb keeps rubbing the base of his ring finger.

It's something you do when you're nervous.

The officer moves closer to your door.

"We just need to establish which one of you belongs here."

If this happens, there are seven rules you need to follow. And the fourth one explains why you should never help him answer that question.

Rule number one. Keep your identification on your side of the door.

The officer will ask for your driver's license.

He'll suggest sliding it through the mail slot. You won't even have to open up.

It will sound reasonable. You'll want this mistake cleared up so you can go back to bed.

Keep the card in your hand.

When you refuse, he won't become angry. He'll simply turn toward the patrol car and make a small gesture.

The man in the back seat will raise something to the window.

It's another driver's license.

You can't see the photograph, but you recognize the corner. Years ago, you accidentally caught yours in a car door. A small piece of the plastic broke away.

The card outside has the same missing piece.

You look down at the one you're holding.

For a moment, you'll feel an overwhelming need to open the door and put the two cards beside each other.

The officer seems to know.

"Bring yours out," he says. "We'll see which one is genuine."

Then the man in the car lowers his card.

He keeps his hand raised against the glass.

His fingers are spread exactly like yours.

Rule number two. If the officer asks you to repeat something, don't give him a second recording.

He'll explain that his camera isn't picking up your voice through the door.

"Just tell me your full name and say you live at this address."

Stay quiet.

He'll try an easier question.

"Are you here alone?"

Then another.

"Do you understand why we're here?"

Eventually, his radio will crackle.

You'll hear your own voice coming through it.

"I live here."

It's the sentence you said when he first arrived.

Then it plays again.

This time, something is wrong. The pause between the words is too long.

"I... live here."

Through the peephole, you'll see the man in the car leaning forward. His lips are moving.

The officer presses a button on his radio.

"Again," he says.

Your voice repeats the sentence.

This time, it sounds right.

The officer looks back at your door.

"Good. Now your name."

Rule number three. If you hear someone you love on the police radio, don't speak to them through it.

After a while, another voice will come through the officer's shoulder speaker.

It's your mother.

She sounds upset.

"Have you found him? Is he all right?"

You'll recognize everything. The way she catches her breath. The small cough she never quite manages to clear.

Your hand will move toward the lock.

Keep it there without turning it.

The officer will say he's been speaking to your emergency contact. She's worried about your behavior. Apparently, you haven't been yourself lately.

"She can explain," he says. "Would you like to speak to her?"

He lifts the radio toward the door.

Your mother says your childhood nickname.

Then she says it again.

The second time, the cough comes in exactly the same place.

You'll hear a quiet click before the sentence starts over.

The officer lowers the radio quickly.

"Poor reception."

Try calling her yourself.

Her line will be busy. Try again, and it will still be busy.

Keep trying when you can. Don't use the radio, even if the officer offers it again.

Rule number four. If he shows you a complaint, read the name of the person who made it.

Eventually, the officer will slide a sheet of paper through your mail slot.

"Perhaps this will clear things up."

At the top is your address.

Underneath is a description of an unknown person refusing to leave the property.

Your approximate height. Your hair color. The clothes you're wearing right now.

You read it twice.

Then you look at the complainant's name.

It's yours.

So is the signature at the bottom, including the little mistake you make in the last letter.

Outside, a car door opens.

The man from the back seat walks up your path.

He's wearing your gray coat. You can see the loose thread hanging from one cuff.

When he steps under the porch light, you finally see his face.

It's almost yours.

The small scar beside his mouth is on the wrong side. His wedding ring sits on the wrong hand. And he's watching the door with an expression you've never seen on your own face.

He looks impatient.

"That's him," he says in your voice. "That's the man in my house."

You look down at the complaint again.

The space marked IDENTITY VERIFIED is empty.

The officer takes a pen from his pocket.

"This would be much easier," he says, "if you'd cooperate."

Rule number five. Don't correct anything the other man gets wrong.

The officer will suggest asking you both a few questions.

Things only the resident would know.

The man outside agrees immediately.

"What color are the kitchen tiles?" the officer asks.

"Green," the man says.

Your kitchen tiles are blue.

You almost say it. You may even take a breath.

The man outside stops watching the officer.

Now he's watching the door.

Waiting.

The next question will be more personal.

The name of your first pet. Where you went to school. What your mother calls you when she's worried.

Sometimes the man will answer correctly. Sometimes he'll get close enough that you'll want to explain the difference.

Don't.

When you remain quiet, he'll become less convincing.

He'll forget which hand should have the ring. He'll glance at the officer before answering a simple question.

Then the officer will ask about a photograph hanging in your hallway.

You haven't told him there's a photograph there.

"Who took that picture?"

The man outside says, "My mother."

It was your father.

You grip the edge of the table until your hand hurts.

The officer waits.

After a few seconds, he turns off his camera.

"We're getting nowhere," he says.

For the first time all night, the other man looks worried.

Rule number six. Whatever they promise, don't step outside voluntarily.

By now, the officer's patience will appear to be wearing thin.

He'll tell you there's an easy way to settle this.

Come onto the porch. Stand beside the other man. Let the second officer take a proper look at you both.

"Nobody's being arrested."

Then he'll say something you need to listen to carefully.

"Once you've left the property, the homeowner can decide whether to let you back in."

The other man will take a key from his coat.

Your key.

He'll hold it near the lock, waiting for you to turn yours from the inside.

Don't touch it.

The officer may threaten to force the door. He may count down from ten.

Let him reach zero.

There won't be a kick. There won't be a crash.

You'll hear him breathing on the other side.

Then the second officer will get out of the car.

She'll walk up the path carrying a clear plastic bag.

Inside are a wallet, a phone, and a small bunch of keys.

You recognize the bright orange key ring. It belongs to the man who lives two houses down.

"That one came outside," she says.

The first officer motions for her to be quiet.

She looks at your door.

"This one heard you, didn't he?"

Neither of them says anything else.

For a long time, all three stand on your porch.

Then the man wearing your coat puts his key away.

"We should try her," he says.

The officer switches his camera back on.

Rule number seven. When they leave, keep your door locked and call your family again.

You'll watch them return to the car.

The man with your face gets into the back seat. Before he lowers his head, he looks toward your bedroom window.

You keep watching until the car turns the corner.

Then you try your mother again.

This time, she answers almost immediately.

"Thank goodness. Are you all right?"

You tell her to lock her doors.

There's a pause.

"What did that officer say to you?"

Your hand tightens around the phone.

She tells you he called earlier. He said somebody had broken into your house and was pretending to be you.

He asked a few questions to make sure he had the right family.

Your first pet. Your school. The photograph your father took.

You close your eyes.

Then you ask whether she told him anything else.

"Just the address," she says. "He said he'd bring you here while they sorted everything out."

You tell her nobody is bringing you anywhere. You're still at home.

You hear her television go quiet.

Then, very softly, she says your name.

There's knocking at her door.

You shout for her to leave it closed.

She says she's only going to look through the window.

You hear her slippers crossing the floor. The little scrape of the curtain rings.

For several seconds, she doesn't speak.

Then you hear her start to cry.

"He looks just like you."

You tell her about the scar. The ring. Anything she can use to see the difference.

She takes a shaky breath.

"The ring's on the correct hand," she says. "And the scar..."

She stops.

Through her phone, you hear a familiar voice outside.

Your voice.

"Mum, please. It's cold."

It sounds tired. Frightened. Exactly the way you sounded when you called her after your first car accident.

You tell her to come away from the door.

The officer says something you can't quite hear.

Then your mother asks, "Why does he have your coat?"

You turn toward the hook beside your front door.

It's empty.

You remember hanging the coat there when you got home.

For the first time, you wonder how long the man in the car had been sitting outside before the knocking started.

At the other end of the call, your mother says, "Let me speak to him for a second."

"You're speaking to me now."

But she's already moving the phone away from her mouth.

You hear the officer clearly this time.

"Ma'am, before you open up, can you confirm that you recognize your son?"

You keep saying her name.

Outside her house, the other you begins to cry.

Your mother whispers, "Of course I do."

Then you hear her key turning.

So, if a police officer ever knocks and asks you to prove you live in your own house, keep the door closed and be careful what you tell him.

And warn your family while you still can.

Because the person waiting in that patrol car is learning how to be welcomed home.
