That's Mister Whistlestop. A bus-safety cat from a 1994 kids' show. Tonight, he took my little brother. All he needed was his name.

And I gave it to him.

In 1994, every school in Larkfield rolled out the TV cart for one tape. Ride Right with Mister Whistlestop. Channel 6.

A cat, taller than the cardboard bus behind him. Crooked yellow top hat. Silver whistle. And a grin too wide for his face... that never moves. Not even when he talks.

"Write your name, your school and your bus on your card! Hold it up to the camera!"

The kids hold up their cards.

"Ride right, kids!"

The tape was pulled that fall. Nobody at Channel 6 remembers why.

My brother and I live with Grandma Della. She's driven bus 9 for thirty-two years, and parks it in our driveway. "Brakes, mirrors, names," she says. "That's the job."

Every kid gets a good morning, by name. She won't pull away until a porch light blinks back.

But one kid never gets a good morning. Seat twenty-two. A boy in a yellow raincoat, even in June. Every day he says, "Hi, Mina."

I never asked his name. Nobody sits with him.

Tomorrow, my brother Sami starts first grade. He's six. All week, he's practiced roll call. Say his name, and he shouts, "HERE!"

I gave him walkie-talkies, so he can always reach me.

Today, I posted his first-day chalkboard. Sami. First grade. Larkfield Elementary. Bus 9. Larch Street. Forty-one likes. And one comment. No face on the account. Just a crooked yellow hat.

"Great smile!"

Grandma's face went grey. "Take it down."

I didn't.

Every year, the night before the first day, Grandma tapes seven rules inside the front door. Every year, nothing comes. This year, she also whispers our family word.

Rule number one.

At midnight, turn off every light that faces the street. Take our name off anything he can see from the road. He can only call the names he can read.

Grandma unscrews the letters off our mailbox. She turns Sami's dinosaur backpack to face the wall. Lights off.

Midnight. Air brakes hiss outside. Yellow headlights fill the curtains. Nobody opens the curtains.

Rule number two.

If he calls your name, never answer. Never say "here" to him. If one of us calls you, look first. Only answer a face you know.

Through a gap in the curtains, his bus door folds open. Something unfolds out of it. Like a ladder. Up, and up, and up.

He's at our mailbox.

Flicker. The middle of the lawn.

"Della? Long time!"

"Mina?"

"Sami?"

My hand slaps over his mouth.

From the porch, in Sami's own little voice: "Here!"

A yellow ticket slides under the door, punched through the middle. Sami. Seat four. Last stop, three a.m.

Rule number three.

If anyone says I sent them, they have to say our word. You never say it first. Never say it where anyone else can hear.

I dial 911. No bars. Not since his headlights came.

Grandma grabs her keys. "Bus 9 has a radio to the depot. Lock this behind me."

Halfway there, it goes out.

"...Clever."

Across the yard, the real Grandma is pounding on the glass of bus 9. Trapped in her own cab, our porch broom wedged through her folding doors. "DON'T OPEN THE DOOR!"

So I don't. Not even to run to her.

At the peephole, the grin whispers, "Smart one. I'll ask Sami."

Rule number four.

If the lights go out, find Sami and hold his hand. Don't let go until the lights come back.

Sami pads upstairs for his dinosaur, Rex. "Be right back!" I let him go. My walkie's on the cushion beside me.

Two ten. Every light dies. Total black.

A small hand slides into mine. Cold.

No answer.

From upstairs... Sami. Laughing. Talking to someone on his walkie.

I'm still holding a hand.

A white glove. One knuckle too many. Folded small around my fingers. Too tight.

In his other glove... my walkie-talkie. Pressed to the grin.

Then it's not our word anymore. A word only works while it's secret.

Rule number five.

If he gets inside, lock yourselves in my room. Open the door only for the word.

Grandma's room has the only deadbolt. I sit with my back against it.

"The word's dead, Sami. Nobody opens."

Two thirty-one. Down in the driveway, a flashlight blinks inside bus 9. Three times.

Is she free?

I go to the window. Just for a second.

The hallway is full of him. Bent double. Hat crushed against the ceiling. Gloves flat on both walls.

"Sami?"

Sami's practiced all week. His mouth answers before his head does.

"HERE!"

He takes Sami's hand, like a crossing guard. I grab the other.

The lights blink, and we're on the stairs. Blink. The front hall. Blink. The lawn. Blink. His bus steps.

Sami's hand slips out of mine.

Over the loudspeaker: "Seat's saved, Mina. Right next to him."

Rule number six.

If he takes one of you, don't chase him on foot. Come get me. We meet him at his last stop.

I don't chase him. I run to bus 9.

I rip the porch broom out of its doors and toss it in. Grandma's at the wheel.

"Nineteen ninety-four. My sixth day. He took three kids off my route. At his last stop, if you call a child's name before the three o'clock bell, and they answer you, he has to let them go. If they answer him... they stay. I got two back."

His head comes out of the driver's window, and turns. Backwards. Grinning at us.

The road ahead is empty.

Rule number seven.

Never get on his bus. Not for anybody. Not even for Sami.

Two fifty-eight.
