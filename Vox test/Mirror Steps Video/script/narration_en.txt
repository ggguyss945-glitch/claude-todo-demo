If you draw seven steps on a mirror and an eighth one appears, wipe the glass clean. Don't wait to see who's coming up the stairs. And if you hear somebody you love down there, listen to what they're asking for. My brother had been missing for fourteen years. When I found him, he was still waiting for breakfast.

It's 11:57 at night, and you're back at the summer camp where your brother disappeared. Tomorrow, a demolition crew is tearing down the last three cabins. Your old friend Tess has the keys. You asked her to let you in because your mom still talks about the green sweatshirt Noah was wearing the night he vanished. You figured if you could bring her one thing, you'd bring her that. Your mom texts while Tess is unlocking the gate. "Check the pocket. He always had one of those cinnamon candies in there." Fourteen years, and she can still tell you what was in his pockets.

On the mess hall porch, Tess steps over a crate marked Save for the Museum. Between the slats, you spot the old breakfast bell. You can still see the crack in the rim from when a counselor dropped it your first summer. Tess holds a finger to her lips as you pass the caretaker's house.

Instead, you find a mirror wrapped in a towel under his bunk. You know the towel. Noah used to catch the sprinkler water with it and wring it out over your pillow when you wouldn't get up. The day before he disappeared, you got him back by changing the alarm on his watch. There are seven red steps drawn on the glass. Tess remembers the game. You draw a staircase in lipstick and call for the Queen of Spades three times. If she gets to the top, she grants a wish. Back then, kids used to dare each other to say her name twice. The third time was supposed to be the joke. Tess shines her flashlight on the glass. She sets her phone face up on the windowsill and brushes a thumb over the lipstick. Little red crumbs come away on her skin. Then a fresh red line appears beneath the bottom step.

If that ever happens, there are seven rules. Pay attention to the fourth. It'll tell you why my brother came back in the same sweatshirt he disappeared in.

Rule number one. Count the steps with your finger. You touch the top step. The lipstick smears. So does the next one. Seven little red marks come away on your finger. When you touch the eighth, your nail clicks against clean glass. You tilt the mirror. The eighth step stays level. So does the woman standing on it. She's in a black dress, looking down at something you can't see. Behind her, the glass shows the cabin the way it used to be. There's a row of damp swimsuits hanging over the rail. Somebody has just walked in from the wash house. You can see two sets of wet footprints appear on the floorboards. One set belongs to bare feet. The other is a shoe print, then a bare foot, all the way to Noah's bunk. Tess angles the flashlight toward the floor. On your side, the dust is smooth from the door to the bed. From somewhere below her, a boy says, "Did they ring it yet?" You know the voice immediately. You still have it on an old voicemail where Noah calls you a brat for changing his alarm. He sounds exactly the same. Even the little whistle through the gap in his front teeth. You lean closer. The woman turns her head. A second ago, she was listening to him. Now she's listening to you.

Rule number two. If you see her face, keep your wishes to yourself. Tess tells you to put the mirror down. You try.

And then you see a hand through the railing behind the woman. Three fingers taped together with the blue medical tape your mom made Noah wear after he shut his hand in the car door. He picks at the tape with his thumbnail. He always did that when he had something to say and couldn't figure out how to start. Tess tells you she's ready to go. You try to tell her that the last thing you said to Noah was to shut the window. The bugs were getting in. "I wish I'd had one more minute with him," you say. You meant it for Tess. You've said it a hundred times before. This time, a woman you've never met answers. "You have it." Tess snatches the towel and throws it over the glass. For a second, you see her fingers press through the cloth. Then the towel drops straight down, as if the mirror has fallen away. There's a doorway in the wall where your reflection was. From the other side comes the smell of the camp kitchen. Somebody's already started the coffee.

Rule number three. Leave somebody on your side of the glass. The doorway is barely wide enough to get your shoulders through. Tess catches your sleeve. Behind her, the second hand on the cabin clock jerks backward. The queen says, "There's time for both of you." You tell Tess to stay. You ask her to count down from sixty. Then you step through.

The other cabin still has its curtains. An old battery lantern glows on the windowsill. It's almost light outside. A sprinkler comes on, spits twice, and dies. You remember that sprinkler. It used to soak the bottom bunk if you left the window open. Noah is sitting on his bed with one shoe on. He looks up and says your name like he expected you an hour ago. Then he gets a good look at you. "Why do you look like Dad?" He looks down at his shoe. "What are they making for breakfast?" He holds out a cinnamon candy without looking up. You take it. The wrapper is warm from his pocket. You remember how he always gave you the red ones, even though the red ones were his favorite. Water taps onto the towel under the window. Then a drop comes up off the towel and hangs there in midair. In the doorway behind you, Tess's flashlight is perfectly steady. You can see dust drifting through the beam. Over here, the same drop falls again. From behind you, Tess calls, "Forty-five."

Rule number four. Ask what he wished for. You take Noah's hand and pull him toward the doorway. As soon as he reaches it, his hand jerks out of yours. He's back on the bunk, one shoe on. Same knot, half tied. You pull again. This time you watch his face. For half a second, he looks old enough to be your brother now. Then he's thirteen again. Sitting on the bed, you ask what he said to the woman. "That I wanted to stay until the breakfast bell," he says. "I was gonna run to the bus in my pajamas. I thought it'd be funny." He tells you the woman has been sitting with him because he's scared he'll miss the bus. Every time he asks if it's time to go, she tells him there's still a little while. You ask how long he's known her.

"Since you went to get Mom," he says. You never went to get Mom. You woke up in the bottom bunk. There was a wet towel on the floor, and you could hear someone outside yelling his name. You spent the morning telling every grown-up who asked that your brother would be back for breakfast. He always came back when there was food. Outside, the sprinkler spits twice. Noah reaches for his loose shoelace. "What are they making for breakfast?" he asks. He asked you that a few seconds ago. You realize that for fourteen years, he's been sitting here with his shoe half tied, waiting to hear a bell. You know why he never heard it. The morning he went missing, the director sent the kitchen staff out to help search. Breakfast was canceled. By noon, they'd put everyone on buses home. That was the last day the camp ever opened. The bell went into storage.

Rule number five. Ring the real bell. You shout for Tess to get the bell from the crate on the mess hall porch. She looks over her shoulder at the open cabin door. The porch is just across the path. You've both made that run in bare feet. "Go," you tell her. Her footsteps disappear. Then the woman in the black dress sits down on the bunk beside Noah. He scoots over to make room for her without even looking up. He's done it before. She asks if you'd like another minute. She pats the empty space beside Noah. "I can put you back the way he remembers you," she says. You catch yourself looking. The mattress has a little dip in it where you used to sit to put your socks on. For the first time all night, you understand why a kid might ask to stay here. You could sit down beside him and tell him to leave the window open. You could make the last thing you said to him something better. From the porch, Tess yells, "Twenty!" Noah holds out another cinnamon candy. You can still feel the first one in your fist. You can hear Tess kicking the box apart on the porch. From deeper inside the old camp, a bell rings. Noah reaches for his other shoe. You nearly tell him to hurry. Then you remember the crack in the old breakfast bell. It always rang twice. A clean note first, then a thin little rattle that sounded like somebody had dropped a spoon. The sound from the old camp was perfect. The queen looks at you. The smile goes off her face. Then Tess hits the real bell with the padlock from the cabin door. There's the rattle. Noah drops his shoelace. "Finally," he says.

Rule number six. Let him go first. You shove Noah toward the opening. He gets one bare foot through. This time, it stays there. Tess grabs him from the other side. He yelps. His taped fingers have slipped right out of the blue tape. By the time she pulls him into the room, his sweatshirt cuffs are halfway up his arms. His voice cracks when he says your name. Then it drops so low you barely recognize it. He stares at his hands. They're shaking, and they're bigger than yours. He stands and hits his shoulder on a bunk he could walk under a second ago. The woman calls his name from behind you. Noah covers his ears. He does it so fast, you know he's heard her call him like that before. Tess shouts, "Five!" You reach for the doorway. Behind you, the queen says, "Wait, he forgot his shoe." It's Noah's voice. Thirteen again. For a second, you turn. You've spent fourteen years imagining the last thing he might have said to you, and your body still turns before you can think. You see the shoe beside the bed. You see her bare foot on the step below yours. Then Tess takes you by the collar and pulls.

Rule number seven. Cover anything else that can reflect the stairs. You hit the floor. Tess shoves the towel into your hands. You wipe from the top down. Through the last streak of red, you see a woman lift her foot toward a step you've already erased. It hangs there for a second. Then the glass goes dark.

Noah is sitting on the floor in a sweatshirt two sizes too small. He's older than you now. You give him your jacket. He holds it by the collar and asks if Mom is still up. It's just after midnight. Of course she is. You haven't called to tell her you're on your way home. When you reach for your phone, your fingers won't open at first. You've been holding the candy so tight that the wrapper left a mark in your palm. Your mom picks up on the first ring. "Did you find the sweatshirt?" Noah holds out his hand for the phone. He says, "Hi, Mom." There's a long pause. Then he says it again, softer, and you hear her chair scrape back. She asks where he is. He looks at you for help. As far as he knows, she dropped him off here a week ago. Behind you, Tess picks up her phone from the windowsill. It was lying face up the whole time. She wipes the dust off the dark screen with her sleeve. "Did you get lipstick on this?" she asks. You count seven little steps on the screen. You touch the bottom one. Your nail clicks against clean glass. From the phone, in the voice your brother had when he was thirteen, somebody asks, "Can I have one more minute?"
