# Script formula: "In this world…" Roblox shorts at 2:00-2:30

This is our reusable template. It is built from the five reference analyses in `analysis/` (r1 Awake At Night, r2 Porch Night, r3 Uh Oh, r4 Tooth Fairies, r5 Clown Gate) and from `docs/NICHE_NOTES.md`.

- **We borrow mechanics only.** Never take their rules, creatures, characters, lines or plots. A reference line quoted below is there as evidence for a device, not as material.
- **The endings are designed, not measured.** All five copies are trimmed: we saw 111-157 s of each, and every ending is missing. Section 10 is therefore built from their setups and our own design.
- **The pace is set for the owner, not copied.** The references are read at 243-268 wpm, which means a sped-up or synthetic voice. The owner records our voice-over himself, so the word budget in section 2 is set for a human read.

---

## 0. The formula on one screen

The niche notes' four steps, stretched to a 2:15 master:

| Step | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Rule | 0:00-0:04 | One sentence on the first frame, with the rule shown happening |
| 2. Consequence | 0:04-0:21 | How people live with it, a mystery line, and the rule within the rule (lock and key) |
| 3. One person | 0:21-0:30 | "I" enters, with a relationship at stake and an innocent planted object |
| 4. Twist 1 | 0:30-0:37 | A **plot event** that changes the story. Not a reframe, not a tone jolt |
| Longer-version beats | 0:37-1:55 | Main reveal, then dilemma, then a failed attempt, then twist 2 (the plant pays), then a choice made by gesture |
| Ending | 1:55-2:15 | Payoff, then a time jump, then a generational mirror, then a last line that reopens the story |

**Numbers to hit:**
- **Length:** 420-450 words, read at 190-200 wpm.
- **Sentences:** median 6-7 words; 35-40% of sentences are 1-5 words.
- **Information:** a new fact or image every ≤ 2.2 s.
- **Turns:** first twist by 0:36, then a turn every 7-10 s.
- **Pauses:** 3-4 planned pauses of 0.6-1.0 s.
- **Cast and dialogue:** dialogue is 4-8% of the words; at most 3 named characters.

**What this tells us:**
1. **Twist 1 is late in all five.** The niche notes say ~30 s, but no reference has a plot event there. At 30 s they hold:
   - a plant (r1 Dex the foil; r4 the drawings);
   - a reframe (r2 "only one rule" plus the Harpers);
   - a tone jolt (r3 the red-eyed baby);
   - a soft personal turn (r5 Dad's notches).

   This is the main structural fix in our formula.
2. **The pace is impossible for a human.** The references average about 4.3 words per second with no air. A natural read is 3.0-3.3 words per second, so we write fewer words. We also make the sentences slightly shorter and let the pictures carry more.
3. **Everything else is remarkably constant across the five:**
   - median sentences of about 7 words, a third of them at 5 words or fewer;
   - a new fact about every 2 s;
   - one shot per sentence;
   - the first-person kid narrator entering at 21-36 s;
   - the rule within the rule at 16-29 s;
   - a turn every 7-10 s once the story moves.

## 2. Word budget for the owner's read

| Words | at 180 wpm | at 190 wpm | at 200 wpm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 400 | 2:13 | 2:06 | 2:00 |
| 430 | 2:23 | 2:16 | 2:09 |
| 460 | 2:33 | 2:25 | 2:18 |
| 490 | 2:43 | 2:35 | 2:27 |

Add about 3 s for the planned pauses.

- **2:15 master:** 420-450 words, read at 190-200 wpm. That pace sounds like an excited kid telling a story, not an announcer.
- **2:30 stretch:** 470-490 words. Spend the extra 15 s on beat 9 (one more attempt) and beat 12 (the ending). See section 3.
- **In the edit:**
  - Trim breaths longer than 0.35 s, except the planned pauses.
  - An optional speed-up of 1.05-1.10x keeps the voice natural. Never go above 1.15x.
  - If the read is long, cut words. Do not cut the planned pauses.
- **Why fewer words is fine:** the references spend roughly 560 words on 130 s. We match their *information rate*, not their word count. We do it with a shorter society block, fragments for reactions, and one picture per noun, so the eye gets a new image even when the voice is slower.

**Where the beats fall, as a share of 2:15:**
- twist 1 at 22-27% (the references are at 30-55%);
- main reveal by 38%;
- twist 2 at 59-73%;
- emotional peak at 72-82% (the same zone as r1, r4 and r5);
- ending at 85-100%.

**The 2:30 stretch (+15 s, +45-50 words):**
- Beat 9 gets one more attempt or a set piece (+8 s).
- Beat 12 gets one more epilogue line, or a sting before the last line (+7 s).
- Do not lengthen beats 1-6. The first 37 s stay the same at any length.

**Open loops to keep running:**
- **One spine loop**, opened in beat 2 and paid in beat 7, then paid again in full in beat 11.
- **Two to four medium loops:** the companion's fate, the planted object, the lock.
- **Micro loops** paid within 1-5 s ("A castle." after "building something").

At any moment, at least two questions must be open. Every 15 s block ends on a question.

## 4. Narration voice

**Person and tense map:**

| Time | Person | Tense | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00-0:21 | "you" (3-6 times) + communal "we/our town" ("We call it…") | present (the law of the world) | Put the viewer inside the rule before any character exists (all five) |
| 0:21 on | first-person kid narrator, "I" | present for portraits ("My mom's face…"), **past from the hinge** ("Then last night,") | Past-tense first person quietly promises the narrator lives to tell it (r2, r4) |
| Twist lines | "you" may return for one line | present | r5's main twist is said to "you": "He's not measuring you." |
| Ending | first person, the narrator's real age revealed | past, then present | r4 "That was 20 years ago." |

**Sentence length and rhythm:**
- **Mix:** median 6-7 words; 35-40% at 1-5 words; 8-10% at 1-2 words (reaction fragments such as "I froze.", "Home?", "Click."). At most 10% of sentences at 16+ words. Use **one** travelling sentence of 20-28 words, at the spectacle only.
- **Length follows tension:**
  - Rule and reveal blocks run 10-13 words per sentence.
  - The dilemma runs 5-7.
  - Panic and the climax run 3-5.

  r2 drops from 20 words a sentence in the hook to 4.4 when Dad knocks; r4's reveal block averages 5.1.
- **End-weighting.** The payload word comes last: the noun the caption and the cut land on ("…every porch is empty", "…it means undo"). Write the stress word last so the reader can land it.
- **Connective openers in 12-19% of sentences, each one a hinge:**
  - "Then" marks the inciting incident and drives the climax.
  - "But" appears **once**, on twist 1.
  - "So" marks cause or agency ("So I went to…").
  - "And" chains the escalation.

  Do not start three sentences in a row with the same opener unless it is deliberate anaphora.
- **Anaphora in threes** for lists: "No more X. No more Y. No more Z." / "Past five feet, past six, past…" / "Some kids… Some families…".
- **The "not just X" shape:** use it at the rule, at twist 1 and at the emotional reframe (r4 uses it 3 times).

**Words:**
- About 75% of the words should be one syllable (r2 has 77%). Prefer concrete nouns the camera can show.
- **Numbers:** small counts are intimate (one, two, three, "both fists"). Use **one big scale number** repeated three times, first as a fact and the last time as emotion (r4 "10,000 years", then "ten thousand years tired"). Captions show numbers as digits.
- **Sound words** get their own caption beat ("Click.", "tink") and give the sound designer a hit point.
- **Avoid:** abstract words ("mysterious", "terrifying"), filler ("You see," before the rule lands; r5 is flagged for it), and one adjective repeated more than 3 times (r4 uses "tiny" 6 times).

**Dialogue:**
- 4-8% of the words, about 15-35 words in 3-6 quoted lines. Every line is a turn or a reveal; no small talk.
- At most 2 speakers besides the narrator. The narrator acts every voice, pitched up for characters (r2, r3).
- The companion may stay **silent** and speak through body and objects (r4 Fern never speaks and is the most moving character in the set).
- Speech tags: "she said" at most 3 times. Captions colour-code speakers (r2: Dad blue, Mom pink).

**Names and pronouns:**
- At most 3 named characters (r2's nine names in 105 s overload the viewer). Everyone else is a role: Mom, Grandpa, the baker.
- The creature is "it" unless it is the companion.
- Never use "he" for two different characters in one scene (r5's clown and Dad).

**Read-aloud rules for the owner:**
- No sentence needs a breath in the middle except the travelling one; about 12 words is the limit before a comma.
- Use contractions (it's, didn't, I'd). Avoid sibilant pile-ups and tongue-twisters.
- **Script markup:** CAPS = stress word; `/` = 0.3 s breath; `//` = planned 0.6-1.0 s pause; *(whisper)* or *(shout)* for voice.
- **Planned pauses (3-4 per script):** after the twist 1 line, after the main reframe line, before the sacrifice gesture, and before "That was N years ago." Everywhere else the gaps stay under 0.35 s. The references have none of these pauses; this is our deliberate fix.

## 5. The first-frame rule

**Four proven shapes:**

| Shape | Template | Seen in | Our example (from IDEA_BANK) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Myth or ritual remix (the owner's favourite) | "In this world, [familiar childhood thing] doesn't just [what we think]. It [hidden purpose]." Hold the key noun for one beat | r4 | "In this world, grandparents don't die. They turn into garden gnomes." |
| B. Condition + consequence | "In this world, if you [ordinary act] when [condition], [consequence]." End on the consequence word | r1, r2 | "In this world, if you whistle after dark, something whistles back." |
| C. Familiar saying made literal | Take a phrase every kid has heard and make it true (r5 swapped one word of a funfair sign) | r5 | "In this world, if the wind changes while you're pulling a face, it stays like that. Forever." |
| D. Power system | "In this world, your [power or fate] is [a universal thing everyone did]" | r3 | (use sparingly; r3 owns the "first word" version) |

**Constraints:**
- **8-17 words.** The references use 12-19, but our read is slower, so the sentence must finish by about 4-5 s. The impossible word lands by 3 s.
- **One concrete, showable noun** in the sentence, and the first visible frame shows it happening: a creature, a prop or a body. All five open on the creature or the rule's image (the doll face, the porch chair, the flying kid, the fairy with a tooth, the clown).
- Put "you" or "your" in it, or make the consequence universal ("every porch").
- The last word is the punch: "empty", "forever", "back". Caption it alone.
- **Never** open with setup and deliver the rule at 7 s (r5). Never put "You see," before the rule.
- Optional micro-loop: withhold the noun and pay it within 1-2 s ("…building something with them." then "A castle.").
- Optional book-end: restate the rule as a command at about 0:20 and end the world block on a visual full stop (r1 "When you hear the music box, be asleep." as the windows go dark).

## 6. Society / consequence block (0:04-0:21)

- **3-5 short parallel facts in 7-10 s**, one shot each. Mix:
  1. a law or in-world sign;
  2. a daily habit;
  3. a class or social angle (the rich pay, the old go first);
  4. one humour beat;
  5. one dark beat.
- **One proof case that kills the obvious loophole:**
  - r2's Harpers locked themselves in, and the house was empty anyway.
  - Viewers' first objection is "why not just hide or cheat?" Answer it here in one line.
- **Every fact must return later on screen** (r2 is the model: the old lady, the tied boy, the paid kid and the knocking all come back). Facts that never return are throwaways (r3's fire hands and dog girl; r1's "same lullaby"; r5's Mrs. Pell).
- **For a myth remix the block can be lore** (r4: scale, visibility, mystery). But give it at least two lines that show people living differently because of the rule. r4's weak spot is that nobody's life changes.
- **Close with the rule within the rule, in two parts:**
  - part A is what the climax cannot do (the lock);
  - part B is how it finally can (the key).

  Plant it here, at least 60 s before it is used. r1's grandpa rule, introduced 9 s before use, is the convenient-solution mistake.

## 7. The one person (0:21-0:30)

- **First "I" by 0:23.** The references enter at 21-36 s, so 0:23 is earlier than all but r4.
- **Introduce with 3 things in 3-5 sentences:**
  1. **A count or a routine** that can break or run out: "I've lost 19…" in r4; "every night for five years" in r1.
  2. **A relationship at stake:** the companion, a parent or a friend. Name at most one of them.
  3. **A trait tied to the rule:** r1's "best fake sleeper alive"; r3's unknown power.
- **The innocent plant:** a list of 3 concrete items (r4 "A star, a tower, a little door.") or a visual-only detail never mentioned (r1 Dex's onesie at 30.7 s, named at 120.6 s). It must look like charm, not like a clue.
- **Optional micro-memory** that makes the bond feel years deep (r4 "I stayed up to meet her when I was six.").
- **Optional foil or mirror** who is the rule's natural victim (r1 Dex), planted for dramatic irony.

## 8. Twist 1: timing and type

**Land it between 0:30 and 0:36**, after a one-line hinge ("Then last night,", "Then came the…", "So yesterday I…"). It must be a **plot event**. The references' 30 s beats (a reframe, a tone jolt, a plant) are not enough on their own.

| Type | What happens | Reference shape (late) |
|---|---|---|
| Routine break | The thing that always happens doesn't, or something extra comes | r4 "But at midnight, it wasn't just Fern at my window." (38.7) |
| Loss | Someone we just met is gone | r1 "In the morning, Dex's bed was empty." (45.6) |
| The rule hits the hero | The rule happens to us, or fails for us | r3 "And everything went backwards." (71.4) |
| Moral shock | A trusted person makes a terrible choice | r2 "And she looked at me." (88.0) |
| Personal reversal | The data or the body turns against a loved one | r5 "…then they start going down." (39.6) |

Then add **one pause**. The next line opens a new question within 2 s.

## 9. What fills 0:37-1:55

These are the building blocks. Use 4-5 of them, in roughly this order, with a turn every 7-10 s:

1. **Main reveal of the hidden mechanism** (beat 7). A new location or creature, described piece by piece with one simile per piece (r1 60-79 s). The hero's ordinary thing is central (r4 "Your tooth… is the last brick."). End on the reframe line.
2. **The rule as the hero's power: the lock bites** (beat 8). The antagonist cannot do the one thing (r4 "The queen didn't move. She couldn't."). This turns the conflict inward, into a choice.
3. **Shrinking stakes** (beat 8). World, then objects, then one loved one.
4. **Failed attempt or loophole** (beat 9):
   - Use loop compression for repeats (r3: tell it once in full, then halve the words each time, then jump with a number: "By the 20th try").
   - Or a mirror character who shows the bad outcome about 20 s early (r2 Danny).
   - Or win becomes trap (r5 "Another clown.").
   - Show any loophole working on someone else first (r1 the sleepwalking kid).
5. **Helper's silent appeal** (beat 10 opener). The companion asks with body and objects (r4: drooping wings, tapping the lid).
6. **Plant pays as twist 2** (beat 10). Repeat the list and extend it; the new items carry the meaning. The reveal reframes a loved one's motive ("She wasn't just leaving me drawings. She was telling me…").
7. **Inverted callback of a vow or pet name** (r2 "Nothing gets in here, buddy." comes back as Dad breaking in, then "I'm sorry, buddy."). It is the strongest single knife in the set. Use it when the twist involves a protector.
8. **Choice by gesture plus the key** (beat 11). An action, not a speech (r4 "I opened my fist."). Then:
   - the travelling sentence;
   - the sound word;
   - the spectacle;
   - a diminishing final image.

**Banned in the middle:** a descriptive stretch longer than 12 s with no new question (r1 86-114 s is the drop-off risk). Also banned: a solution introduced less than 30 s before it is used.

## 10. The ending pattern (1:55-2:15)

This is designed. All reference endings are trimmed; this order fits their setups and r4's visible epilogue.

1. **Resolve the main question on screen.** Never depend on a part 2 (r2's weak spot).
2. **Diminishing image** after the spectacle. One small thing left: a light, a laugh, a lantern.
3. **Time jump:** "That was 20 years ago." Choose a number that echoes one in the story (r4: tooth number 20 becomes 20 years). This reveals the narrator's real age, and the kid voice was an adult remembering.
4. **Generational mirror:** last becomes first, the child becomes the parent, the same ritual one generation on (r4 "Last night, my daughter lost her first tooth.").
5. **The last line reopens the story:**
   - it contradicts an earlier "never" or "no more" (r4: "no more tooth fairies, ever", then "there was a gold coin and a tiny…");
   - or it replays the first-frame rule with a new meaning.

   This is the sequel sting. It is optional; resolution is not.
6. **Loop-friendly last frame** (our recommendation, not observed). The final image or line rhymes with frame 1, so the auto-replay feels like a continuation.

## 12. What the owner's favourite (Tooth Fairies) does differently

| r4 does this | The others | Our rule when we write the r4 way |
|---|---|---|
| **A familiar childhood myth with a hidden mechanism.** Teeth are bricks, the castle is a ship. The viewer already knows half the world, so the hook is instant and personal ("your teeth") | r1, r2 and r5 invent a creature or law; r3 invents a power system | Pick a ritual, myth, saying or object every viewer has lived. Reveal the machine behind it in stages: purpose, then destination, then the cost |
| **First-person kid narrator who is secretly an adult.** "I've lost 19…", then "That was 20 years ago." | Kid narrators, but no age reveal in the visible part | Keep the narration in past tense with no adult tells; reveal the age at the time jump |
| **A small object collected over years becomes the key.** 19 drawings in a shoebox are planted at 27-34 s as charm and become one picture and a message at 87-99 s | r3's sandwich and r1's crank are props, not collections | Have the hero keep a collection with a count. Show 3 items early; at the payoff, repeat those 3 and add the 2 that carry the meaning |
| **The choice is between keeping what you love and letting it go.** The tooth in the fist (keep Fern), or open the hand (she goes home) | r1, r2, r3 and r5 are survival, rescue or puzzle stories | The rule's lock must make keeping possible, and the key must make letting go a free act ("it has to be given") |
| **Gentle antagonist; the conflict moves inside.** The Queen says "Take your time." | External threats | Remove the clock at the dilemma so the choice is moral, not forced |
| **Silent companion.** Fern never speaks; wings droop; she taps the lid | Helpers who speak, or none | The companion asks with gestures and objects only |
| **Wonder before threat; bittersweet spectacle.** Night turns to day, then "just one tiny light" | Fear-driven retention | Add **one real threat or cost** anyway; r4's low danger is a weakness (see below) |
| **Set dressing foreshadows the reveal.** Ceiling stars, rocket and SPACE posters | Signs that state the rule | Hide the answer in the hero's room before twist 2 |
| **The 20-years-later ending.** A generational mirror, plus a contradiction cliff ("no more fairies", then a new coin and drawing) | Endings missing | Section 10 |

**Fix r4's weak spots when we copy its shape:**
- Its twist is late (38.7 s and 48.1 s). Land ours by 0:36.
- Its society block is thin. Add at least 2 lines of people living differently.
- It has low danger. Add one threat (a creature, a clock or a cost).
- Its logic leaves "why this kid's tooth?" unanswered. Answer "why me?" in one line.
- It gives the quiet beats no air. Use the planned pauses.

## 13. Mistakes to avoid (with the evidence)

1. **First twist after 0:36.** All five references are late (38.7-86.5 s), and the stretch from 21 to 35 s is the softest in r1 and r4.
2. **A breathless read.** The references run 243-268 wpm with no pause longer than 0.8 s, so "Dex's bed was empty", "I froze." and "Slow." get no air. Plan 3-4 pauses and write fewer words.
3. **Rule after the first sentence.** r5 opens with setup, puts "You see," before the rule, and lands it at 7.2 s.
4. **A convenient solution.** r1's grandpa rule arrives 9 s before use. Plant the key at least 60 s earlier, inside the rule within the rule.
5. **Logic holes the comments will find:**
   - "Why did nobody try this in 90 years?" (r5)
   - "It never takes sleepwalkers, so why does it attack?" (r1)
   - "Why is this kid's tooth the last brick?" (r4)
   - "The Harpers rule says the house is taken…" (r2)

   Pre-answer the one obvious objection in one line.
6. **Throwaway world-building and dangling plants.** r3's power gags never return. Unpaid in the visible parts: r1's "same lullaby", r3's toe kid, r5's Mrs. Pell and the rising arm. If a fact does not come back, cut it.
7. **A slow descriptive middle.** r1 86-114 s runs five 12-18-word sentences with no new question. Cap description at 12 s and end it on a question.
8. **A passive foil or helper.** r1 Dex is a costume with no choice; r3 Leo has no line until 110 s. Give the companion one active gesture at the peak.
9. **Cast overload.** r2 names nine characters in 105 s. Name at most 3.
10. **Pronoun ambiguity by ear.** r5's "he" switches between Dad and the clown.
11. **Too dark for the audience.** r2 has a father shoving the mother and throwing his child out. Keep deaths and losses off-screen and keep violence out of the family.
12. **An ending that depends on a part 2,** or a missing generational payoff (r2 risk).
13. **A low-curiosity title.** "Porch Night" is weaker than a forbidden action ("Never Be Caught Awake At Night") or a mystery word ("Uh Oh"). Use 2-4 words that are a forbidden act, a mystery object or a saying.
14. **Picture and line mismatches.** r5 says "ten" over a "12 FEET" plate. r4's captions misspell "everyone" and "fern". Check every caption and every number against the shot.
15. **Over-repeated adjectives** (r4 "tiny" ×6) and filler openers ("You see," three times in five scripts).
16. **Copying.** Any reference rule, creature, plot beat or line. Search YouTube Shorts for the rule phrase before we script.

## 14. Draft checklist (a draft must pass every line)

**Hook (0:00-0:10)**
- [ ] The sentence begins "In this world," (or "In this town,") and states the rule in 8-17 words. The impossible word lands by 3 s, and the last word is the punch.
- [ ] Frame 1 shows the rule happening (the creature, prop or body), with the punch word captioned.
- [ ] Any withheld noun or micro loop is paid within 2 s.
- [ ] A mystery line (the spine loop) is spoken by 0:12.

**World (0:10-0:21)**
- [ ] There are 3-5 facts showing people living with the rule, each with its own shot, including one joke and one dark note.
- [ ] The obvious loophole ("why not just…?") is answered in one line or one proof case.
- [ ] The rule within the rule has two parts (lock and key), is spoken by 0:21, and decides the climax.
- [ ] Every world fact returns later on screen.

**Person and twist 1 (0:21-0:37)**
- [ ] The first "I" lands by 0:23, with a count or routine, one relationship at stake and a trait tied to the rule.
- [ ] An innocent plant (a list of 3, or visual-only) is in by 0:30 and pays at least 50 s later.
- [ ] Twist 1 is a plot event by 0:36, after a hinge line, followed by a pause.

**Middle (0:37-1:55)**
- [ ] The main reveal of the hidden mechanism lands by 0:55 with one quotable reframe line.
- [ ] After twist 1 there is a turn every 7-10 s, and no stretch longer than 12 s has no new question.
- [ ] The hero tries something that fails or costs before the solution.
- [ ] Twist 2 at 1:20-1:40 pays the plant by repeating and extending it.
- [ ] The choice is between keeping and letting go (or a real sacrifice), made by gesture, using the key.

**Ending (1:55-2:30)**
- [ ] The main question is resolved on screen, followed by a diminishing image, a time jump with an echoed number, a generational mirror, and a last line that reopens or recontextualises.

**Numbers**
- [ ] 420-450 words (470-490 for 2:30). Read once with a timer at 190-200 wpm: it comes in under 2:30.
- [ ] Sentence median 6-7; 35-40% at 1-5 words; at most 10% at 16+ words; only one sentence over 20 words.
- [ ] Openers And/But/So/Then in 12-19% of sentences; "But" used once, on twist 1.
- [ ] Dialogue is 4-8% of the words; at most 2 speakers besides the narrator; every line is a turn.
- [ ] At most 3 named characters; no ambiguous "he" or "she".
- [ ] 3-4 planned pauses are marked `//`; stress words are in CAPS.

**Picture**
- [ ] Every sentence has its shot, and every new noun in a long sentence gets a cut. The mean shot is 1.3-1.8 s.
- [ ] The one big number is used three times at most, shown as digits in the captions.

**Safety and originality**
- [ ] Kid-safe: losses off-screen, no family violence, the creature is cute-creepy and not gory.
- [ ] Nothing from the references (rules, creatures, lines, plots). The rule phrase has been searched on YouTube Shorts and nothing close was found.
- [ ] The title is 2-4 words: a forbidden act, a mystery object or a saying. It does not spoil the twist.

**Read-through test before recording:**
